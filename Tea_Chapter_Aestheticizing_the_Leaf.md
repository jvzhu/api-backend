# Aestheticizing the Leaf: Literary, Visual, and Cultural Consumption of Tea in East Asia

## Keywords

*Camellia sinensis*; Tea Culture; East Asian Literature; Visual Arts; Material Culture; Cultural Consumption; Ritual; Aroma; Tea Utensils; Aestheticization; Japanese Tea; Authenticity

## Abstract

This chapter theorizes the cultural transformation of *Camellia sinensis* from botanical matter into an aesthetic and symbolic medium in East Asia. Rather than treating tea as a passive commodity absorbed into pre-existing cultural practices, it approaches tea as a materially and sensorially active participant in processes of cultural production. Tracing practices of tea preparation and appreciation from early modern traditions to the contemporary period, the chapter examines how flavor, aroma, processing, brewing techniques, and teaware mediate relationships among botanical materiality, human agency, and cultural meaning. Drawing on literary treatises, visual representations, and material artifacts, it asks how cultivation, processing, tasting, and ritualization transform plant matter into aesthetic experience and embodied knowledge. Tea thus emerges not merely as an object of consumption but as a medium through which distinctions of taste, status, identity, and cultural sensibility are articulated and negotiated. The chapter develops a material-cultural account of aestheticization, demonstrating how the material affordances and sensory qualities of tea have shaped interconnected literary, visual, ritual, and consumer cultures across East Asia.

## Potential Reference / Research Note

### Yukitada Shimamura on Rai San’yō, Sencha, and *Sanshi Suimeisho*

**Lecture:** “Steeped in Art and Nature: On Rai San’yō’s ‘Sanshi Suimeisho’”  
**Speaker:** Yukitada Shimamura (島村幸忠), PhD; scholar of early modern Japanese literati culture and sencha history; professional sencha practitioner; faculty member at Osaka University of Economics.  
**Organizer:** Council on East Asian Studies, Yale University.  
**Venue:** Sterling Memorial Library, International Room (177), Yale University.

**Relevance to the chapter:** This lecture is a potential reference for the chapter’s argument that tea functions not merely as a consumable commodity but as an aesthetic medium embedded in literary, visual, spatial, and social practices. Shimamura’s discussion of Rai San’yō’s *Sanshi Suimeisho* presents **sencha** as part of a late early-modern literati environment in which poetry, art, nature, sake, and tea were mutually constitutive elements of cultivated life. His use of Sinitic poems by Rai Kyōhei in *Jūjun kagetsu* and related works held at Yale’s Beinecke Library may provide a useful historical case for examining how tea’s sensory and material properties become mediated through literary production, artistic appreciation, domestic/architectural space, and ritualized sociality.

**Possible connection to the chapter’s theoretical framework:**

> botanical materiality → sensory experience → tea preparation and teaware → literati sociability and spatial practice → literary/visual representation → aestheticization

**Potential use:** Consider citing Shimamura’s scholarship on Rai San’yō and sencha as a concrete example of how tea’s material affordances and sensory qualities are transformed into aesthetic and cultural meaning. The case may also help substantiate the chapter’s discussion of **Japanese tea, material culture, ritual, literary culture, and aestheticization**.
